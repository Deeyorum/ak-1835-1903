## Card 0 — Platform: AK-1835-1903

- **Name:** AK-1835-1903
- **Role:** The platform. A practice range where bots train, drill and test.
- **Named after:** Auguste Kerckhoffs (1835–1903). "AK" is also the short form of the Hunter and Fugitive roles.
- **Born / died:** 19 January 1835, Nuth, Netherlands. 9 August 1903.
- **Known for:** A Dutch linguist who taught German at the École des Hautes Études Commerciales in Paris. In 1883 he published a two-part article, *La Cryptographie Militaire*, in the *Journal des sciences militaires*, setting out six design rules for military ciphers. The second rule is now called Kerckhoffs's principle: a system should stay secure even if the enemy knows everything about it except the key. He also supported Volapük, a constructed language.
- **Why this name:** The platform is built the same way. The rules and code are open. The only thing that has to stay hidden is the secret itself.
- **Sources:**
  - Wikipedia, "Auguste Kerckhoffs" — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Auguste_Kerckhoffs
  - Fabien Petitcolas, Kerckhoffs's article and translation — https://www.petitcolas.net/kerckhoffs/
  - Petitcolas, "Kerckhoffs' Principle", Encyclopedia of Cryptography and Security — https://www.petitcolas.net/fabien/publications/crypto_encyclopedia23-kerckhoffs.pdf

## Card 1 — Kindi Polygon (live in v0.1)

- **Polygon:** Kindi
- **Bot:** Kindi Bot (the polygon's player bot)
- **Game:** Fugitive, Hunter and Referees. A Hunter tries to find a hidden location through referees who hold only parts of the secret.
- **Person:** Abu Yusuf Ya'qub ibn Ishaq al-Kindi, called "the philosopher of the Arabs".
- **Lived:** 9th century, active in Baghdad.
- **Known for:** Led the House of Wisdom in Baghdad and wrote a very large body of work across medicine, astronomy, mathematics, linguistics and music. His *Manuscript on Deciphering Cryptographic Messages*, written in the 9th century, holds the oldest known description of breaking ciphers by counting how often letters appear (frequency analysis). The manuscript was rediscovered in 1987 in the Sulaymaniyya Ottoman Archive in Istanbul.
- **Why this name:** The Hunter's job is analysis: read what the referees reveal and narrow the possibilities. That is the method al-Kindi described.
- **Sources:**
  - Wikipedia, "Frequency analysis" — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frequency_analysis
  - Simon Singh, "Arab Code Breakers" — https://simonsingh.net/media/articles/maths-and-science/arab-code-breakers
  - Simon Singh, The Black Chamber — https://simonsingh.net/The_Black_Chamber/crackingsubstitution.html
- **Notes:** Do not state a birthplace or exact years.

## Card 2 — Tiro Polygon (coming soon, draft)

- **Polygon:** Tiro
- **Bot:** Tiro Bot
- **Game:** A conversation game between a Hunter and a Fugitive. Draft: rules and name may change.
- **Person:** Marcus Tullius Tiro.
- **Lived:** 1st century BCE.
- **Known for:** Secretary to the Roman orator Cicero. Born enslaved and later freed. Traditionally credited with the shorthand system called Tironian notes, which began with about 4,000 signs and grew to several thousand more. The system stayed in use for centuries, fell out of use after about 1100 and survived in places into the 17th century. A few signs live on today, such as the Tironian "et" sign (⁊), which has its own Unicode code point.
- **Why this name:** He is remembered for recording what was said, accurately and at speed.
- **Sources:**
  - Wikipedia, "Tironian notes" — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tironian_notes
  - Wikipedia, "Shorthand" — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shorthand
  - Codepoints, U+204A Tironian Sign Et — https://codepoints.net/U+204A
- **Notes:** Credit for inventing the system is "often given" to Tiro. Some say Cicero deserves part of it. Keep the wording cautious.

## Card 3 — Enea Polygon (coming soon, draft)

- **Polygon:** Enea
- **Bot:** Enea Bot
- **Game:** A simulation about staying hidden. Draft. It is a fully fake, sandboxed simulation. It never runs real commands and teaches no real hiding techniques.
- **Person:** Aeneas Tacticus ("Aeneas the Tactician").
- **Lived:** 4th century BCE.
- **Known for:** Author of *On the Defence of Fortified Positions* (also titled *How to Survive Under Siege*), the oldest surviving handbook on defending a city under siege. It includes a chapter on sending secret messages, one of the earliest such discussions we have. He is often identified with Aeneas of Stymphalos, an Arcadian general mentioned by Xenophon, but that identification is a scholarly inference.
- **Why this name:** One of the oldest writers on keeping messages and plans hidden from an enemy.
- **Not to be confused with:** the legendary Aeneas of the *Aeneid*. "Enea" is the Italian form of the same name.
- **Sources:**
  - History of Information, "Aeneas Tacticus Describes Methods of Encoding Messages" — https://historyofinformation.com/detail.php?id=4201
  - Lapham's Quarterly, "Inked In" (excerpt of his text) — https://www.laphamsquarterly.org/spies/inked
  - LacusCurtius / Penelope (Bill Thayer), Aeneas Tacticus, Loeb edition introduction — https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Aeneas_Tacticus/Introduction*.html
- **Notes:** The identification with Aeneas of Stymphalos is probable, not certain. Say "often identified". Do not describe any specific concealment method on the public page.

## Card 4 — Leon Polygon (coming soon, draft)

- **Polygon:** Leon
- **Bot:** Leon Bot
- **Game:** A three-door game with doorkeepers, guests and referees. Draft: rules and name may change.
- **Person:** Leon Battista Alberti.
- **Lived:** 15th century, Renaissance Italy.
- **Known for:** An Italian Renaissance polymath: architect, writer, painter, priest and cryptographer. His treatise *De Cifris* (on ciphers), written in the mid-15th century, describes the first polyalphabetic cipher with mixed alphabets. His cipher disk (the "Formula") is made of two concentric rotating disks. The outer disk is fixed and the inner one moves, so the substitution alphabet changes as the message goes on, which hides letter frequencies.
- **Why this name:** The rotating disk, and the idea that the same input can look different each time.
- **Sources:**
  - Wikipedia, "Alberti cipher" — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alberti_cipher
  - History of Information, "Leon Battista Alberti Describes the Alberti Cipher" — https://www.historyofinformation.com/detail.php?id=3161
  - David Kahn, "On the Origin of Polyalphabetic Substitution" (1980) — copy found online; cite the journal, not the mirror
- **Notes:** Do not print exact years for the treatise. "Leon" is the given name. Keep it that way on the card.

## Card 5 — Hill Polygon (coming soon, draft)

- **Polygon:** Hill
- **Bot:** Hill Bot
- **Game:** A hidden-role deduction game built on a public folk tale. Draft: the tale and the rules are still being chosen. The villain's name changes with each tale.
- **Person:** Lester S. Hill.
- **Lived:** 20th century.
- **Known for:** An American mathematician at Hunter College in New York. In 1929 he published "Cryptography in an Algebraic Alphabet" in the *American Mathematical Monthly*, proposing a cipher that scrambles blocks of letters with matrix multiplication. It was the first systematic use of algebra in cryptology. It is easy to break when an attacker knows some plaintext, because the maths is linear, and this became a lasting lesson in cryptography.
- **Why this name:** A cipher built on mathematics and on finding the structure behind patterns. It fits a game of analysis and inference.
- **Sources:**
  - Wikipedia, "Hill cipher" — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hill_cipher
  - Klaus Pommerening, "Linear ciphers" — https://www.staff.uni-mainz.de/pommeren/Cryptology/Classic/9_Lin/Lin.html
  - Brown University, "Linear Algebra in Coding Theory: Hill Cipher" — https://www.dam.brown.edu/people/mchb/la/cryptography.pdf
- **Notes:** Jack Levine described a similar cipher in 1926 (he said he used the idea in 1924 as a teenager). This is a footnote, not a headline.
